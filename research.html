Research & Pedagogical Foundations

Why learning spaces matter, what makes their use worthwhile, and how teachers can learn to harness their affordances.

Our central principle

Beyond a change of venue

Learning beyond the classroom is not automatically better. A space becomes pedagogically valuable when teachers deliberately use its distinctive features to help students observe, investigate, interact, create or understand in ways that are difficult or less authentic in the classroom.

What can students meaningfully do here that they could not do as well in the classroom?

1. Theories that explain why space matters

Foundational theory

Ecological affordances

Gibson (1979) describes affordances as opportunities for action arising from the relationship between an environment and an actor. A garden's plants, surfaces and changing conditions offer possibilities, but these only become learning opportunities through appropriately designed tasks.

SPACE application: Identify the distinctive features of a space, then connect each to an intended student action and learning outcome.

Spatial professional learning

Teacher spatial competency

Mahat & Loh (2024) studied a professional learning and action-research programme in a Singapore secondary school. The case study reported improvements in teachers' spatial knowledge, self-efficacy and intentionality.

SPACE application: Combine learning about space with collaborative dialogue, authentic trials and reflection—not just a briefing about available facilities.

Teacher collaboration

Spatialised professional learning

Charteris & Smardon (2018) discuss teacher learning through spatialised practice, including peer learning, coaching and exchange of practices in new-generation learning environments.

SPACE application: Ask teachers to examine and redesign authentic lessons together, then share evidence of how the environment shaped learning.

Read open-access paper ↗
Learning mechanism

Cognitive apprenticeship

Collins, Brown & Holum (1991) describe modelling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection and exploration as methods for making expert reasoning visible.

SPACE application: A facilitator thinks aloud while choosing a space: What is the objective? What affordance matters? What would students actually do? Is the added value worth the effort?

2. What affordance-driven teaching looks like

Example: P3 Science — Diversity of Plants

Space as venue

Bring students to the Eco-garden, seat them together and ask them to classify plants using photographs on a worksheet.

The location changes, but the task does not meaningfully use the garden.

Space as pedagogical resource

Students examine actual plants, compare observable characteristics, document variation, and use their observations as evidence for classification.

The garden supplies authentic, variable specimens that are central to the investigation.

This is an illustrative design comparison, not an experimental finding that the second lesson necessarily produces better outcomes.

3. How teachers can learn this competency

The following sequence is a proposed SPACE intervention synthesising the literature; it is not a published, validated programme.

  1. Notice: Guided walkabout and affordance mapping. Teachers identify what each space enables or constrains. Gibson (1979).
  2. Compare: Analyse two well-designed versions of a lesson and explain when leaving the classroom adds value. Experiential and reflective learning.
  3. Model: Facilitator uses a think-aloud to demonstrate spatial-pedagogical decision-making. Collins et al. (1991).
  4. Design: Teachers collaboratively redesign an existing curriculum lesson around specific affordances. Charteris & Smardon (2018).
  5. Trial: Teach in the selected space; observe student interactions and collect learning evidence. Mahat & Loh (2024).
  6. Reflect and transfer: Evaluate whether the space genuinely supported learning; adapt the approach to another topic or space. Reflective professional inquiry.

4. A decision guide for teachers

Before taking students out, ask:

  1. Affordance: What distinctive opportunity does this space offer?
  2. Alignment: How does that opportunity serve the learning objective?
  3. Activation: What will students actually do with the space's features?
  4. Value: Is the learning benefit likely to justify the extra time, movement and management?
  5. Evidence: How will we know the intended learning occurred?
Choosing the classroom is also a sound decision when another space adds no meaningful learning value.

5. What the evidence can—and cannot—claim

Supported: Spatial professional learning can develop teachers' knowledge, confidence and intentional use of learning environments, as illustrated by the Singapore case study.

Not established: That moving lessons outside necessarily improves achievement, or that a proposed SPACE web module alone will change practice.

Evaluation implication: Measure changes in teachers' reasoning and lesson design, then examine student learning evidence. Do not use frequency of out-of-class lessons as the only success measure.
